# What past Space Apps winners tell us

Researched 2026-10-01. Sources at the bottom.

## How judging works

Every level of judging scores each project **1–5** on five criteria:

| Criterion | What judges check | How MicroFire Atlas answers it |
|---|---|---|
| **Influence** | Quality and scale of impact | Crew fire safety for Moon/Mars missions; researchers find comparable NASA tests in seconds |
| **Creativity** | Originality | Evidence-first ranking that shows where data *stops* (Gap Map), not a "risk predictor" |
| **Validity** | "Does your math make sense, did you use the data sets correctly?" | Every value traces to a NASA table and page; units normalized with tests; derived values labelled |
| **Relevance** | Meets the challenge goals | Brief: "AI-powered dashboard that **summarizes, ranks, and interprets**" → AI summary, Mission Relevance ranking, cited interpretation |
| **Presentation** | Communication | 7-slide or 30-second demo; Story Mode on the site |

## What gets submitted

- A **demo**: either **7 slides** or a **30-second video**, at a public link with no login.
- A **final project link**: website or repo, public, no login.
- The Story Mode on the site can run 60–90 s, but the *submitted* video must fit in 30 s. Script both.

## Closest past winner

**2025 Best Use of Science — SpaceGenes+** (one person, Berlin), for "Build a Space Biology Knowledge Engine". That challenge asked for "a dynamic dashboard that leverages artificial intelligence, knowledge graphs, and/or other tools to summarize a set of NASA bioscience publications". Its wording is almost the same as our 2026 brief.

NASA's description of why it stood out:

- **One sharp scientific question**: how radiation and microgravity *together* affect astronaut health at the molecular level. It did not try to be a generic search box over all publications.
- A named audience and use: "researchers and mission planners", to "identify important molecular changes".

**Lesson for us**: lead with one sharp question the data can actually answer. Ours: *"At a given oxygen level, what airflow keeps a flame alive in microgravity, and where does NASA's evidence run out?"* BASS-II Table A.1 answers it directly. One PMMA film shows three different outcomes at ~16.5 % O₂ depending only on flow: tests B16 (quenched), B20 (burned the whole sample) and B19 (blew off).

## Other patterns across 2024–2025 global winners

- **Real NASA data, used visibly**: e.g. *WaterWise* (2024 Best Use of Data) used NASA OpenET evapotranspiration data, and *Resonant Exoplanets* (2025 Best Use of Data) ingested JWST spectra.
- **A decision for a named user**: farmers, mission planners, satellite operators. Not just "explore data".
- **Risk and decision framing works when it's transparent**: e.g. *Astro Sweepers* (2025 Galactic Impact) built a risk index, and *SkySense* (2025 Best Use of Technology) gave risk assessments and safest time windows. Our equivalent is Mission Relevance plus Evidence Confidence. Keep them explainable, and never call them certified risk.
- **Storytelling wins its own award** (2024 TerraTales, 2025 HerCode Space). A strong Story Mode is a second path to a prize.
- Small teams win: SpaceGenes+ was one person. Focus beats headcount.

## Competitor scan (same 2026 challenge, public repos)

Several public repos for "Flame in Freefall" already exist, e.g. FlameScope, Fotia ("predict fire behavior outside the known earth environment"), ZeroG-Ignis, IGNIS and Fire-atlas. A common framing is AI that *predicts* fire behaviour. Our differentiation: **test-level NASA records with page citations**, explicit outcomes (quench / blow-off / sustained), and an honest gap map.
